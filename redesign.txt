CAMP-01 — Tenant & Institution Architecture
Design specification v1.0
 Foundation domain
 India-first · Multi-tenant · Multi-institution


We are now designing the first actual domain of the Campus Operating System.

I want to approach this as if we were preparing a specification for a team that will implement, test, deploy and maintain this system for years.

That means we need more than a list of tables. We need to define the real-world concepts, their relationships, business invariants, lifecycle, ownership boundaries, failure conditions and the contracts other modules will depend on.

CAMP-01 must solve one fundamental problem: How do we represent any educational institution, its organizational structure, campuses and institutional relationships without forcing every institution into the same administrative model?

1. Scope and responsibility
CAMP-01 owns the institutional identity and structure of the platform.
CAMP-01 domain boundary
Tenant Management

Customer boundary, lifecycle, provisioning, configuration ownership

Institution Management

Institution identity, classification, registration identifiers, verification

Campus & Location Management

Physical sites, virtual campuses, addresses, institutional campus relationships

Organizational Structure

Administrative units, departments, placement offices, reporting structures

Institutional Relationships

University affiliation, constituent colleges, management groups, institutional networks

Institutional Configuration

Time zones, locales, operational defaults, branding and configuration versions


Explicitly outside CAMP-01

Student admission, academic records, faculty employment records, program curricula, examination, attendance, placement rules and financial transactions. CAMP-01 provides the structures these domains reference.


This boundary matters.

For example, CAMP-01 knows that an institution has a Computer Science Department. It does not own which students are enrolled in that department. That belongs to the academic and student lifecycle domains.

Similarly, CAMP-01 knows that a Placement Office exists. It does not own placement drives or recruitment activities.
1.1 Domain ownership rules
Information	Owner	CAMP-01 responsibility
Tenant identity	CAMP-01	Full ownership
Institution identity	CAMP-01	Full ownership
Campus records	CAMP-01	Full ownership
Administrative hierarchy	CAMP-01	Full ownership
Academic organizational units	CAMP-01	Structural identity only
Programs	Academic Structure	Reference and association only
Student identities	Identity / Student Lifecycle	No student record ownership
Placement office	CAMP-01	Organizational unit
Placement drives	Placement	No drive ownership
Institutional settings	CAMP-01	Full ownership
User permissions	Identity & Authorization	No permission ownership

This means other modules reference CAMP-01 records using stable identifiers. They must not create competing copies of institutional structure.

2. First critical decision: what exactly is a tenant?
This is probably the most consequential decision in the entire foundation.

Let's distinguish four concepts:
Concept	Meaning
Platform	Our overall software ecosystem
Tenant	A customer-level data isolation and administration boundary
Institution	An educational institution represented within the platform
Campus	A physical or operational site associated with an institution

A tenant is not necessarily a college.

A tenant is the boundary within which data is managed under a particular customer's administrative and security model.
2.1 Why this distinction matters
Consider three scenarios.
Scenario A — One university, several campuses
Tenant Alpha


University Alpha
Campus A


Campus B


Campus C





One tenant is sensible when the university shares administration, security governance and data-management responsibilities across campuses.

Scenario B — One educational trust, multiple colleges
Tenant Beta


Education Group
College A


College B



Separate institutional identities; shared services can be selectively enabled.


The colleges can share selected operations through explicit authorization without automatically sharing every record.

Scenario C — Two independently operated affiliated colleges
Tenant Gamma

College C
Independent boundary


Tenant Delta

College D
Independent boundary



Public affiliation relationship only

Shared University Directory Reference

Neither college gets access to the other's private student, administrative or placement records merely because they are affiliated with the same university.

2.2 Our tenant design rule
I recommend this rule:
A tenant should represent the highest level at which an organization legitimately shares data governance, security administration and platform ownership.

We should allow:
- One tenant containing multiple institutions.
- One institution containing multiple campuses.
- Multiple institutions sharing a physical location.
- Multiple institutions belonging to one management group.
- Separate tenants referring to the same public university.
- Cross-institution collaboration through explicitly authorized integrations.
We should not automatically allow:
- Cross-tenant data access.
- Shared student records between affiliated institutions.
- Inherited administrative access merely because two colleges belong to the same trust.
- Shared policies without an explicit adoption mechanism.
Architectural decision ADR-C01-001
Tenant is the primary security isolation boundary; Institution is the principal educational and organizational boundary.

This becomes a foundational invariant.

3. Build an institutional identity model
Now we need to define what an institution actually is.

An institution has several identities:
1. Its legal or formally recognized identity.
2. Its public-facing identity.
3. Its platform identity.
4. Its affiliations and external registrations.
5. Its internal organizational structure.
These may not be identical.

For example, a college may commonly operate under a shorter public name while its registered legal name is different.

We must support both without creating duplicate institutions.
3.1 Institution entity
The central Institution entity should have:
Attribute	Purpose
institution_id	Stable internal identifier
tenant_id	Owning tenant
institution_code	Tenant-unique operational code
legal_name	Formal registered name
display_name	Public-facing name
institution_type	University, college, institute, etc.
ownership_type	Government, private, public-private, etc.
status	Institutional lifecycle
directory_entry_id	Optional public directory reference
timezone	Institutional default
default_locale	Institutional language/locale default
created_at	Creation timestamp
updated_at	Last update timestamp

Do not use institution names as identifiers.

Institutions can change names. Their internal IDs must not change.
3.2 Institution classifications
We should avoid one enormous enum containing every possible Indian institution category.

There are several independent classification dimensions.
Classification	Possible values
Institution kind	University, college, institute, autonomous college
Ownership	Central government, state government, private, other
Governance	Public, private, aided, unaided
Recognition status	Applicable regulatory recognition states
Institutional role	Degree-awarding, teaching, constituent, affiliated
Delivery model	Physical, online, hybrid

Not every classification applies to every institution.

For example, a college might be private and unaided, while a university might be government-run.

These attributes must not be mashed together into one institution_type value.

We should use controlled taxonomies with room for extension, rather than arbitrary user-generated labels.
3.3 Institutional identifiers
Different institutions can have multiple official and operational identifiers.

Examples include:
- AISHE identifiers.
- UGC-related references.
- AICTE approval references where applicable.
- State department identifiers.
- Examination-board or university affiliation codes.
- Internal institution codes.
These must be stored as a collection, not as columns on the institution table.

Proposed entity: InstitutionIdentifier.
Field	Description
identifier_id	Internal ID
tenant_id	Tenant
institution_id	Institution
scheme	Identifier scheme
value	Identifier value
issuer	Issuing authority
valid_from	Start of validity
valid_to	End of validity
verification_status	Verification state
evidence_document_id	Supporting document reference

Do not assume that every scheme has the same uniqueness rules.

A unique identifier may be unique across a national registry, while another may only be unique within a particular state or authority.

We should therefore maintain an identifier-scheme registry containing its scope, validation rules and uniqueness characteristics.
3.4 Public institutional directory
There is another interesting problem.

Suppose two colleges subscribe independently but are affiliated with the same university.

We should not need to store two entirely separate public identities for that university.

I recommend separating:

Platform Institution Directory

A platform-level registry containing verified public institutional references.

Tenant Institution

The private institutional workspace used by a customer.
Platform institution directory

Verified public name, location, official references

Tenant A

Private institution workspace
Institution A


Tenant B

Private institution workspace
Institution B



Both can reference the same public directory entry


Important: a directory reference must not imply shared administration or permission to access another tenant's records.

4. Campus and location architecture
This deserves more attention than it initially appears to.

Many conventional systems simply create a campus_id field on the institution.

That works for basic cases, but introduces problems when:
- A university has several campuses.
- A campus hosts multiple institutions.
- A college operates from multiple buildings or locations.
- A university provides programs across different sites.
- Online or distance-learning environments exist.
- A campus is shared but administration is independent.
4.1 Separate campus from physical address
A campus is not just an address.

A campus is an operational site with an institutional purpose, lifecycle and configuration.

A location represents where something physically exists.

One campus may span several physical sites, and a physical site may host multiple institutional entities.

I would initially model:
- Campus
- PhysicalLocation
- InstitutionCampus
- CampusLocation
University Institution


Campus North

Primary campus


Campus South

Secondary campus




North campus locations

Academic block, administration block, hostel


South campus locations

Engineering block, research centre




4.2 Campus entity
Field	Description
campus_id	Stable ID
tenant_id	Tenant
campus_code	Tenant-unique code
campus_name	Campus name
campus_kind	Physical, virtual, distributed, etc.
lifecycle_status	Planned, active, closed
timezone	Campus-specific timezone
default_locale	Campus-specific locale
operational_metadata	Limited extensible configuration

4.3 Institution-campus relationship
Instead of making every campus permanently owned by exactly one institution, use an association entity.

InstitutionCampus
Field	Purpose
institution_id	Institution
campus_id	Campus
relationship_type	Main, branch, constituent, shared or other
is_primary	Whether primary campus
valid_from	Start
valid_to	End
status	Active/inactive

This supports multiple associations while preserving history.

A campus may be associated with more than one institution where legally and operationally appropriate.

A constraint should prevent two simultaneously active primary-campus assignments for the same institution unless the institution's configuration explicitly allows a primary-campus set. My recommended default is one primary campus.
4.4 Physical locations
A separate PhysicalLocation can represent:
- Building.
- Block.
- Street address.
- City.
- District.
- State.
- Postal code.
- Country.
- Geographical coordinates.
- Location type.
We should make this reusable for later domains like classrooms, examination centres, libraries, hostels and placement interview locations.

However, we should not prematurely build a comprehensive indoor navigation or room booking system into CAMP-01.

5. Organizational hierarchy architecture
Now we come to one of the most complex aspects.

Institutions rarely follow one universal organizational hierarchy.

Consider two institutions.

Institution A:
University
 ├── Academic Affairs
 ├── Placement Office
 ├── Finance
 └── Administration


Institution B:
College
 ├── Principal's Office
 │    ├── Administration
 │    └── Finance
 │
 ├── Academic Division
 │    ├── Engineering
 │    └── Management
 │
 └── Central Career Development Cell


We should support both.

But there's another issue: one department may report to multiple functions, or a shared placement cell may support multiple schools.

A rigid parent_department_id field cannot represent every relationship.
5.1 Proposed design: organizational nodes + typed relationships
Use two independent concepts.

OrganizationalUnit: Represents an organizational entity.

OrganizationalUnitRelation: Represents its relationship with another organizational entity.

Examples of organizational unit types:
Category	Examples
Executive	Chancellor's office, principal's office
Academic	School, faculty, department
Administrative	Registrar, administration
Operational	Placement cell, examination cell
Financial	Accounts office, finance department
Support	IT department, student support
Research	Research centre, laboratory
Other	Configurable institutional units

These types should be configurable and extensible, but governed by a controlled type registry.
5.2 Relationship types
I recommend initially supporting:
Relationship	Meaning
CONTAINS	Parent structurally contains a unit
REPORTS_TO	Administrative reporting relationship
SUPPORTS	One unit supports another
SHARES_SERVICES_WITH	Shared operational services
COORDINATES_WITH	Coordination relationship

Not all relationships should be interpreted as hierarchy.

For example, a Placement Office can support several departments without becoming their parent.
5.3 Visual example
Institution


Academic Division
CSE Department

ECE Department



Administration
Finance

Registrar




Central Placement Office

Supports both CSE and ECE placement operations

Structural containment and operational support are different kinds of relationships.
5.4 Important hierarchy constraints
We must enforce:
1. Organizational units must belong to one institution.
2. Containment edges must not cross institution or tenant boundaries.
3. A containment hierarchy must be acyclic.
4. A unit should have at most one primary structural parent at any given time.
5. Multiple non-hierarchical relationships are allowed.
6. Historical structural changes must be preserved.
7. Deactivating a unit must not destroy its historical relationships.
8. Every active unit must have a valid institution association.
Why only one primary containment parent?

Because a structural hierarchy should be navigable and unambiguous.

Additional operational relationships can express matrix reporting and shared services without making the organizational tree impossible to interpret.
5.5 Should academic units be separate?
Yes.

This is an important architectural decision.

An organizational department and an academic department may be related but shouldn't be assumed to be the same thing.

For instance, one academic school may be managed by a larger administrative unit. An interdisciplinary research centre may involve several academic departments.

Therefore:
- OrganizationalUnit belongs to the administrative organization model.
- AcademicUnit belongs to the academic structure model.
- Their association should be explicit.
CAMP-03 will define academic units, but CAMP-01 must allow an academic unit to map to an appropriate organizational unit where necessary.

We should never use the organizational hierarchy as the sole source of academic truth.


6. Institution groups and institutional relationships
These might look like the same thing, but they solve different problems.

An institution group represents an organizational or management grouping.

An institutional relationship represents a specific relationship between two institutions or between an institution and an external authority.
6.1 Institution groups
Consider an education trust that operates:
- An engineering college.
- A management college.
- A pharmacy college.
- A nursing institute.
They may share some administration, infrastructure or services, but each institution has its own identity.

Proposed entities:

InstitutionGroup
Field	Description
group_id	Internal identifier
tenant_id	Tenant
group_code	Unique group code
group_name	Public/administrative name
group_type	Trust, society, educational group, network
status	Lifecycle
description	Optional description

InstitutionGroupMembership
Field	Description
group_id	Group
institution_id	Member institution
membership_type	Owned, managed, operated, associated
valid_from	Start date
valid_to	End date
status	Current relationship status

Cardinality:
\[
InstitutionGroup \quad M:N \quad Institution
\]

An institution can belong to multiple groups where appropriate, such as a management group and a wider educational consortium.
Critical access rule
Group membership must not automatically grant access to institutional data.

For example, a central trust administrator may be allowed to see financial summaries for all its colleges but not individual student disciplinary records.

The group provides the organizational relationship. CAMP-02 authorization defines the access.

These two concerns must remain separate.
6.2 Institutional relationships
Now consider a private engineering college affiliated with a state university.

The college and university are separate institutions, and they have a formal affiliation relationship.

Other relationships include:
Relationship type	Example
AFFILIATED_TO	College affiliated with a university
CONSTITUENT_OF	College is constituent of a university
MANAGED_BY	College managed by an education trust
PART_OF_GROUP	Institution belongs to a group
MERGED_INTO	Institution legally merged into another
SUCCESSOR_TO	Institution succeeded another institution
COLLABORATES_WITH	Institutional collaboration

Relationships should have:
- Source institution.
- Target institution or public directory reference.
- Relationship type.
- Start and end dates.
- Verification status.
- Authority/evidence reference.
- Source and last verification time.
We should not assume that these relationships create an administrative hierarchy.

For example, AFFILIATED_TO is not the same as CONSTITUENT_OF.

Their semantics, legal meaning and operational implications are different.
Internal and external relationships
We should support two cases:
1. Internal: Both institutions belong to the same tenant. They can reference each other using tenant-scoped IDs.
2. External: The other institution belongs to another tenant or exists only in the public directory. Reference its public directory identity without establishing access to its private workspace.
This avoids cross-tenant foreign-key dependencies and accidental data-sharing relationships.

7. Institutional configuration architecture
An institution has many configurable characteristics.

Examples:
- Default language.
- Timezone.
- Date and number formatting.
- Branding and logo.
- Student-facing contact details.
- Default campus.
- Supported notification channels.
- Institutional website.
- Operational preferences.
We should have a centralized configuration system, but we need to avoid turning it into an unrestricted key-value database.
7.1 Configuration hierarchy
I propose the following resolution model:
Platform defaults
       |
Tenant defaults
       |
Institution settings
       |
Campus settings
       |
Effective configuration


This hierarchy applies only to settings explicitly designated as inheritable.

It must not be used to override security boundaries, data isolation or mandatory platform constraints.
7.2 Configuration categories
Category	Examples
Identity presentation	Logo, colors, institution display name
Localization	Timezone, locale, date formats
Contact	Official email, phone, website
Operational defaults	Default campus, preferred language
Notifications	Allowed channels and preferences
Integrations	ERP configuration references
Interface	Branding and dashboard preferences

Important distinction:
- Configuration defines how the institution operates.
- Policies define what is permitted.
- Workflows define how a process executes.
These should not live in one generic configuration document.

For example, the minimum CGPA required for campus placements is not an ordinary institutional setting. It belongs to the placement policy domain.
7.3 Configuration versioning
Every major configuration change should preserve:
- Previous value.
- New value.
- Effective time.
- Editor identity.
- Approval status, where required.
- Change reason.
For technical implementation, we can support validated JSON configuration documents for extensible settings, with a schema and namespace per setting family.

Avoid storing core structural data such as institution IDs, campus relationships and organizational hierarchy inside JSON.

9. Database design — first physical representation
We should make a deliberate distinction between a conceptual entity model and an actual relational database.

A good entity relationship diagram does not automatically produce a good production schema.

We need:
- Strong foreign-key constraints.
- Tenant-aware composite relationships.
- Temporal history.
- Unique identifiers.
- Controlled classification values.
- Data integrity.
- Indexes aligned with actual query patterns.
9.1 PostgreSQL schema organization
I propose logical database schemas:
platform.*
    tenant
    tenant_subscription
    tenant_provisioning

institution.*
    institution
    institution_identifier
    institution_group
    institution_group_membership
    institution_relationship
    institutional_address
    institutional_contact

campus.*
    campus
    institution_campus
    physical_location
    campus_location

organization.*
    organizational_unit
    organizational_unit_relation

configuration.*
    institution_configuration
    campus_configuration

registry.*
    institution_directory_entry


The directory is a shared platform capability, with tightly controlled write permissions.

The schema split helps organization, but it is not the security boundary. The application and database policies must enforce the tenant scope.
9.2 Core DDL example
The following is a starting schema for the most important entities, not a complete migration.

Tenant and institution

    slug VARCHAR(100) NOT NULL UNIQUE,

    created_at TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL DEFAULT NOW()

CREATE TABLE institution.institution (
    tenant_id UUID NOT NULL,
    id UUID NOT NULL,

    institution_code VARCHAR(80) NOT NULL,
    normalized_code VARCHAR(80) NOT NULL,

    legal_name VARCHAR(300) NOT NULL,
    display_name VARCHAR(200) NOT NULL,

    institution_type VARCHAR(50) NOT NULL,

10. Temporal data and institutional restructuring
This is something I particularly want to get right.

Institutional structures change.

An institution may:
- Rename a department.
- Split a school into two schools.
- Merge departments.
- Shift a department between schools.
- Open a new campus.
- Close an existing campus.
- Merge with another institution.
- Change its affiliation.
We must not let these changes destroy historical data.
10.1 Effective-dated relationships
Every relationship that can change over time should generally support:
valid_from
valid_to
created_at
created_by
change_reason
status


Use half-open intervals:
\[
[valid\_from,\ valid\_to)
\]

That means the start date is included and the end date is excluded.

For example:
Department	Parent	Effective period
Computer Science	School of Engineering	2020–2025
Computer Science	School of Computing	2025 onward

The old relationship is closed, not deleted.
10.2 Reorganization example
Suppose an engineering school is reorganized.
Before:
School of Engineering
    |
    +-- CSE
    +-- ECE
    +-- Mechanical

After:
School of Computing
    |
    +-- CSE
    |
    +-- AI & ML

School of Core Engineering
    |
    +-- ECE
    +-- Mechanical


We need to preserve:
- Old organizational identities.
- New organizational identities.
- Effective dates.
- Authorized change records.
- Successor relationships where relevant.
Historical academic records should not suddenly appear to belong to a school that did not exist when those records were created.

The academic domain will maintain historical student assignments. CAMP-01 supplies the historically correct institutional structure.
10.3 Merge and successor semantics
A merge should not mean simply updating the old institution's ID to a new one.

Instead:
- Old institution records become inactive or merged.
- A new or surviving institution retains its own stable ID.
- A MERGED_INTO or SUCCESSOR_TO relationship is recorded.
- Historical references remain intact.
- New transactions use the current institution identity.
The same principle applies to campus and organizational unit closures.

12. Institutional onboarding pipeline
This is the first real-world workflow we will implement using CAMP-01.

A new college must be able to onboard itself or be onboarded by our platform operations team.

We need a guided, repeatable and auditable process.
12.1 Complete onboarding workflow
Institution provisioning pipeline
1. Customer onboarding
   
   Customer identity, tenant request, organizational relationship and initial administrative contact.
2. Tenant provisioning
   
   Platform Core creates the tenant, isolation context and initial subscription/configuration references.
3. Institution registration
   
   Legal name, display name, institution kind, ownership, identifiers and public directory matching.
4. Institution verification
   
   Validate identity claims, relevant recognition/affiliation references and evidence.
5. Campus setup
   
   Create campuses, locations, addresses, timezones and institution-campus relationships.
6. Organizational setup
   
   Configure administrative departments, placement office, academic organizational units and reporting structure.
7. Institutional relationships
   
   Add group membership, affiliation and other verified relationships.
8. Institution configuration
   
   Branding, locales, communication contacts, operational defaults and integration references.
9. Readiness validation
   
   Check missing information, duplicate identities, invalid relationships and incomplete configuration.
10. Institution activation
    
    Approve activation, publish the institutional structure and enable dependent domain onboarding.

12.2 Onboarding must support two operating modes
Mode A — Self-service

An institution initiates registration and provides the required information.

Mode B — Assisted onboarding

Our operations team creates or verifies the institution and imports its initial configuration.

Both modes should use the same underlying domain commands and validation logic.

The difference should be the actor and authorization context, not two separate implementations.
12.3 Readiness validation
Before activation, the system should evaluate a structured checklist.
Validation	Required?
Institution has legal name	Yes
Institution has display name	Yes
Institution code is unique	Yes
Institution type is selected	Yes
Tenant is active	Yes
Institutional owner/contact exists	Yes
Timezone is valid	Yes
Institutional identity has required verification	According to onboarding policy
At least one campus exists	Configuration-dependent
Primary campus exists	If campus-based operations require one
Organizational hierarchy exists	Not necessarily at initial activation
Public directory match exists	No, but unresolved match should be recorded

Not every institution should be forced to provide the same information.

A purely online educational institution, for example, should not be required to create an ordinary physical campus just to become active.
12.4 Partial onboarding failure
Suppose campus setup succeeds but institutional verification fails.

We must not end up with an incoherent half-active institution.

Use a provisioning workflow with explicit intermediate states, transactional boundaries and idempotent commands.

The platform should retain valid onboarding work while preventing unauthorized business operations.

We should not wrap the entire onboarding journey in one enormous database transaction. It may span several days and involve human verification.

Instead, treat it as a durable workflow with compensating actions and clear failure/retry points.

15. Multi-tenancy and security architecture
This deserves its own section because the platform will contain institutional information belonging to different customers.

We need to make accidental cross-tenant access structurally difficult.
15.1 Recommended isolation strategy
I recommend starting with a pooled PostgreSQL architecture with explicit tenant ownership, while keeping the ability to move large or regulated customers into dedicated database deployments later.

Three common models are worth distinguishing:
Model	Description	Trade-off
Pooled	Multiple tenants in shared database/tables	Cost-efficient, requires strong isolation
Silo	Dedicated database/deployment per tenant	Stronger operational isolation, higher cost
Hybrid	Shared infrastructure for most tenants, dedicated environments for selected tenants	Flexible, more operational complexity

Our logical architecture should support all three.

This means every tenant-owned record must have a tenant_id, whether the record is stored in a shared or dedicated database.
15.2 Database-level protection
I would use PostgreSQL Row-Level Security as an additional isolation layer.

Conceptually:
ALTER TABLE institution.institution
ENABLE ROW LEVEL SECURITY;

ALTER TABLE institution.institution
FORCE ROW LEVEL SECURITY;

CREATE POLICY institution_tenant_isolation
ON institution.institution

USING (
    tenant_id =
    current_setting('app.tenant_id')::UUID
)

WITH CHECK (
    tenant_id =
    current_setting('app.tenant_id')::UUID
);


At the beginning of each database transaction:
BEGIN;

SET LOCAL app.tenant_id = '...';

-- Tenant-scoped application operations

COMMIT;


The tenant ID must be derived from the authenticated server-side security context, not trusted from a user-supplied request parameter.

The application database role should not have permissions that bypass RLS. We also need to be deliberate about table ownership, BYPASSRLS, privileged functions and background worker credentials.

Important: RLS is a defence-in-depth mechanism, not the complete authorization system.

We still need:
- Backend tenant verification.
- Organization and institution scope checks.
- Resource-level authorization.
- Cross-tenant access tests.
- Secure background job context.
- Tenant-aware caches.
- Tenant-aware object storage.
- Tenant-aware search indexes.
15.3 Isolation outside the database
A common mistake is to secure PostgreSQL and forget everything else.

We must account for all infrastructure.
Component	Isolation requirement
PostgreSQL	Tenant ownership and RLS
Redis	Tenant-aware key namespace
Object storage	Tenant-scoped storage and authorization
Search	Tenant filters enforced server-side
Message broker	Tenant-scoped event metadata and consumer authorization
Background jobs	Trusted tenant context
Logs	Prevent unnecessary personal and institutional data exposure
Analytics	Explicit permitted aggregation and export boundaries
Backups	Secure storage and controlled restoration

For example, a cache key should not simply be:
institution:123


It should include the appropriate tenant scope:
tenant:abc:institution:123


Similarly, an object-storage URL must not become a permanent bypass around our authorization service.
15.4 Cross-tenant operations
There will be legitimate cases where our platform needs to perform cross-tenant operations:
- Institutional directory matching.
- Platform-level verification.
- Authorized support access.
- Customer migration.
- Shared educational group reporting.
- Explicit data-sharing integrations.
These operations should go through separately authorized platform-level services.

They should not be implemented by disabling tenant restrictions in ordinary application queries.

Support access should be time-limited, purpose-bound and audited.

16. Institutional data governance and auditability
We should distinguish four different types of information.
Category	Example	Treatment
Master data	Institution name, campus code	Authoritative domain-owned record
Relationship data	Institution affiliated with university	Effective-dated relationship
Configuration data	Default timezone	Versioned configuration
Audit data	Institution name changed by administrator	Append-only audit event

These should not be combined into a single generic history table without clear semantics.
16.1 Change history
For important structural changes, store:
- What changed?
- Which record changed?
- Previous value.
- New value.
- Effective date.
- Who requested the change?
- Who approved it?
- When was the operation recorded?
- Why did it happen?
- What downstream systems need to be notified?
I recommend two time concepts:

Valid time: When the change is considered effective in the real institutional world.

Transaction time: When our system learned and recorded the change.

For example, a department restructuring may be effective from 1 July, but the institution may inform us on 10 July.

Both dates matter.

This is the foundation for maintaining historically accurate institutional structures.
16.2 Approval-sensitive changes
Not every update needs approval.

We should define change categories.
Change	Suggested control
Display name	Authorized update
Legal name	Verification and approval
Official identifier	Evidence and verification
Campus opening	Authorized approval
Campus closure	Approval with dependency checks
Institutional merger	Platform and institutional verification
Administrative unit creation	Authorized unit administrator
Hierarchy restructuring	Versioned change and approval where configured
Branding	Institution administrator

The exact approval authority should be configurable for each tenant, with mandatory platform-level guardrails for high-impact changes.
16.3 Data retention and deletion
Institutional history should not disappear just because an institution or campus is archived.

We should distinguish:
- Deactivation.
- Archival.
- Tenant closure.
- Customer-requested export.
- Personal-data deletion.
- Legal or regulatory preservation.
CAMP-01 provides the structural lifecycle. The platform governance and privacy domains determine applicable retention and deletion procedures.

One important principle: archival is not a substitute for a lawful retention/deletion policy.

17. Scalability and performance considerations
This is where we make sure the design can support a large institutional ecosystem.

CAMP-01 itself should not become a high-throughput bottleneck.

Most institutional master records change relatively infrequently compared with student records, attendance and application events.

This is an important workload distinction.
17.1 Expected workload patterns
Operation	Workload characteristic
Institution creation	Low frequency
Campus creation	Low frequency
Organizational hierarchy update	Low frequency, high integrity
Institution directory search	Read-heavy
Effective configuration lookup	Read-heavy
Campus directory lookup	Read-heavy
Historical hierarchy query	Read-heavy
Onboarding/import	Bursty background processing
Audit/event publication	Continuous, modest compared with student activity

Therefore, I would not introduce unnecessary distributed infrastructure inside CAMP-01.
17.2 Performance architecture
- Cache institutional details and hierarchy read models.
- Invalidate caches using versioned domain events.
- Use recursive CTEs or hierarchy projections for tree traversal.
- Index foreign keys, tenant IDs and frequently used scope fields.
- Keep structural mutations transactional.
- Perform large imports asynchronously.
- Use pagination for directory queries.
- Maintain read replicas when justified.
- Avoid rebuilding the complete institutional hierarchy on every API request.
For hierarchy-heavy read workloads, we can eventually materialize a closure table or hierarchy projection.

However, I would begin with adjacency relationships and validated tree traversal. A closure table adds maintenance complexity and should be introduced when query patterns justify it.

18.1 Duplicate institution detection
This is particularly important because Indian institutions can have:
- Similar names.
- Different abbreviations.
- Different registered and operating names.
- Multiple campuses.
- Legacy identifiers.
- Name changes.
We should use a duplicate-detection process based on:
1. Official identifiers.
2. Registered name.
3. Location.
4. Institution type.
5. Affiliation information.
6. Verified public website.
7. Existing directory references.
A fuzzy match should produce a possible duplicate, not automatically merge two institutional identities.
18.2 Institutional structure cycles
Consider an invalid hierarchy:
School A
    |
Department B
    |
School C
    |
Department A
    |
School A


This must be rejected.

Cycle validation should occur in the domain layer before the hierarchy mutation commits.

For concurrent changes, the mutation needs appropriate locking or serialization; two individually valid changes must not jointly create a cycle.
18.3 Historical changes versus current status
We should never assume that:
institution.status = ACTIVE


means every campus, department and institutional relationship is active.

Each entity has its own lifecycle and temporal state.

The effective structure at a requested point in time must be resolved through the relationship records and their validity periods.

I don't want us to test CAMP-01 only by creating a college and checking whether it appears on the dashboard.

19.2 Essential architectural tests
These should exist before CAMP-01 is considered production-ready.

Tenant isolation tests
- Tenant A cannot retrieve Tenant B's institution.
- Tenant A cannot associate a campus belonging to Tenant B.
- Tenant A cannot create an organizational hierarchy using Tenant B's units.
- Manipulating a URL ID does not bypass tenant filtering.
- Cache keys cannot collide across tenants.
- Background jobs cannot accidentally inherit another tenant's context.
Hierarchy integrity tests
- A child cannot be its own parent.
- A containment cycle cannot be committed.
- A unit cannot have two overlapping primary parents.
- An expired relationship is not returned as current.
- A hierarchy change preserves historical relationships.
- Concurrent mutations cannot jointly violate hierarchy constraints.
Lifecycle tests
- An unverified institution cannot be activated.
- A closed campus cannot receive a new active association.
- An archived unit cannot be used for a new assignment.
- A tenant suspension prevents prohibited new operations.
- Archival does not remove historical institutional records.
Integration tests
- An institution activation generates the appropriate event.
- Duplicate event delivery does not duplicate downstream records.
- An unsuccessful event publication is recoverable.
- Consumers handle older supported event versions.
- An institutional name change updates appropriate read models without rewriting historical snapshots.

23. What comes next: CAMP-02 — Identity & Access Architecture
CAMP-01 defines the institution and its structure. Now we need to determine who can operate within that structure.

This is a very large domain in its own right.

CAMP-02 must handle:
- One person having multiple institutional memberships.
- Students and alumni.
- Faculty and non-teaching staff.
- TPOs and placement coordinators.
- HODs, deans, principals and registrars.
- Institution administrators.
- Platform support personnel.
- Temporary and delegated responsibilities.
- Multi-department assignments.
- Committee memberships.
- Approval authorities.
- Scoped permissions and data visibility.
I particularly want us to design a proper authorization model that supports both traditional hierarchical institutions and highly flexible modern universities.

It must answer questions like:

Can one faculty member manage two departments? Can an HOD access a different department's placement data? Can a central TPO see all campuses but modify only particular drives? Can a student also serve as a student representative? Can someone temporarily receive administrative authority without becoming a permanent administrator?

These are not unusual exceptions. They are real requirements our architecture should be able to represent.

CAMP-01 establishes where everything belongs. CAMP-02 establishes who can do what there. CAMP-03 will establish the academic world that operates within it.

That's the foundation we should build on
