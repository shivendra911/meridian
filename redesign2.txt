CAMP-02 — Identity, Membership & Authorization Architecture
Architecture specification v1.0
 Security foundation
 India-first · Multi-tenant


CAMP-01 established the institutional world: tenants, institutions, campuses, organizational units and institutional relationships.

Now we need to establish the people who operate inside that world.

This is arguably one of the most security-critical domains in our entire platform.

I want us to design it for a university with 50,000 students, multiple campuses, thousands of faculty and staff members, hundreds of administrative responsibilities, and a continuously changing organizational structure.

It must also work for a small college with a principal, a few administrators and a single placement coordinator.

The architecture must not become more complicated to use just because it is powerful underneath.

Our primary design principle:
One human identity, multiple institutional relationships, multiple responsibilities, context-aware permissions, and a complete history of who was allowed to do what.


1. Define the CAMP-02 domain boundary
First, we must distinguish five concepts that conventional applications frequently combine.
Concept	What it represents
Identity	Who is this person or system?
Account	How does this entity authenticate?
Membership	What is its relationship with an institution?
Role assignment	What responsibilities has it been given?
Permission	Which operations can it perform, and on which resources?

These are not interchangeable.

For instance, a faculty member does not become a placement coordinator simply because they are a faculty member. They receive an additional responsibility with a particular scope and period of validity.

Likewise, a student representative may be authorized to submit certain requests without receiving access to every student record.

2. The fundamental identity model
Let's start with the real-world question:

What happens when the same person is a student, a student representative, an alumnus, a faculty member and a placement coordinator at different points in time?

A conventional system might create separate user accounts or store several role flags in one table.

Neither is sufficient for our platform.

We need a persistent identity model.

A person may have multiple authentication identities.

Introduce AuthenticationIdentity.

Never use an email address as the permanent identity key. Email addresses can change, be shared administratively, or be reassigned.

Also, do not assume a person is unique merely because two accounts have the same phone number or name.

Identity merging needs an explicit, verified process.
2.4 Global identity versus institution-local privacy
This is an important decision.

We want one person to use the platform across institutions, but we must not turn that into unrestricted cross-institution visibility.

Therefore, we should maintain:
- A platform-level person identity.
- Tenant-scoped person representations or membership references.
- Institution-scoped profile visibility.
- Explicit identity-linking and verification procedures.
The existence of a person in Institution A must not allow users in Institution B to discover that person's membership.

Global deduplication should be a controlled identity-resolution operation, not an automatic lookup available to institutions.
ADR-C02-001
Identity is reusable across institutional memberships, but institutional identity and information remain access-scoped.

3. Membership architecture — who belongs where?
An account by itself does not explain why someone has access to an institution.

We need a membership model.

Consider these examples:
- A student belongs to an institution.
- A faculty member works at an institution.
- An alumnus retains a relationship with an institution.
- A visiting examiner has temporary access.
- An external recruiter has a restricted institutional relationship.
- A parent or guardian may have authorized visibility into a student's information.
These relationships are not all permanent employment or student enrollment records.
3.1 Distinguish membership from business affiliation
We need two separate concepts:

Institutional affiliation: The underlying real-world relationship, such as student enrollment, employment, alumni status or external partnership.

Platform membership: The person's authorized presence within the institution's platform workspace.

For example, someone might have an alumni affiliation but no active platform account. Another person might have a temporary guest membership without being an employee or student.

This distinction prevents an identity system from becoming the source of academic or employment truth.

InstitutionMembership

We should support separate membership scopes for campus-level or institution-level access.

A membership must never imply unrestricted access to the institution.
3.3 Membership types
I propose these starting categories.
Category	Membership types

This should be a controlled taxonomy, not an unrestricted role list.

There is an important distinction between a membership type and a role:
- FACULTY identifies the person's institutional relationship.
- HOD identifies a responsibility the person has been assigned.
- PLACEMENT_COORDINATOR identifies another responsibility.
- VIEW_STUDENT_RECORDS is a permission.
We should never use those interchangeably.

For example, a student can graduate, changing their student affiliation while retaining a separate alumni membership.

Likewise, staff employment can terminate while an alumni or external mentorship relationship remains.


4. Roles architecture — model responsibilities, not job titles
This is where the system becomes interesting.

Indian institutions can have very different titles for people performing similar work.

One institution calls someone a TPO. Another uses Placement Head. Another has Training and Placement Officer. Some have multiple department placement coordinators.

We should support institution-specific titles without breaking the platform's authorization model.
4.1 Three dimensions of a role
Every role should have three characteristics:
1. Role identity: What responsibility does it represent?
2. Scope: Where does that responsibility apply?
3. Validity: When does that responsibility apply?
For example:
Person: Faculty Member A

Role: Placement Coordinator
Institution: Institution X
Scope: CSE Department
Valid from: 01 July 2026
Valid until: 30 June 2027


This role assignment should not grant the person access to ECE or Mechanical department records unless another permission or responsibility explicitly allows that.
4.2 System roles versus institutional roles
We should support two types of role definitions.

Platform-defined roles

Reusable definitions maintained by the platform.

Examples:
- Institution Administrator.
- Principal.
- Registrar.
- HOD.
- Faculty.
- Student.
- TPO.
- Placement Coordinator.
- Alumni Coordinator.
- Examination Controller.
Institution-defined roles

Custom definitions created by an institution.

Examples:
- Deputy Training Coordinator.
- Department Career Champion.
- Student Placement Ambassador.
- Industry Liaison Officer.
- Hostel Warden.
The custom role must be based on the platform permission catalogue and must pass authorization constraints.

A college must not be able to create a role that bypasses platform security protections.
4.3 A role is not a permission bundle forever
We should version role definitions.

Suppose an institution defines a placement coordinator role that initially has five permissions.

Later, management decides it should have seven.

Changing the role should not silently alter historical access decisions.

Introduce:

RoleDefinition
Field	Description
role_id	Stable role ID
tenant_id	Nullable for platform roles
institution_id	Nullable for tenant/platform scope
role_code	Stable operational code
role_name	Display title
role_category	Academic, administrative, student, etc.
role_owner_type	Platform or institution
status	Draft, active, deprecated
current_version	Latest approved version

RoleVersion
Field	Description
role_version_id	Immutable version
role_id	Parent role
version_number	Version

effective_from	Start date
effective_to	End date
approved_by	Approving principal
created_at	Version timestamp

A role assignment should reference a specific role definition version or an explicit version-resolution policy.

My preference: active assignments normally follow an institution's approved current version, but high-risk assignments can be pinned to a specific version. Every authorization decision must record the effective role version used.

5. Role assignment architecture — the real source of authority
A person might hold multiple responsibilities simultaneously.

Therefore, avoid these designs:

users.is_tpo = true
users.is_hod = true


Also avoid:

Both fail once we support multiple responsibilities, scopes and validity periods.

Instead, create a dedicated role assignment entity.

Not every role requires an approval workflow.

A low-risk student club role could be self-service or coordinator-approved.

An institution administrator or principal assignment should require stronger approval, identity verification and separation-of-duties controls.

The role catalogue should define which roles require:
- Grantor authority.
- Independent approval.
- Strong authentication.
- Fixed maximum duration.
- Conflict-of-interest checks.
- Additional verification.
- Periodic access review.
5.4 Assignment and revocation must be immediate enough
Suppose a placement coordinator's assignment is revoked at 10:00.

We cannot wait until the user's authentication token expires several hours later.

The authorization service must recognize that the relevant assignment is no longer valid.

Therefore:
- Role assignments live in the authoritative authorization database.
- Access tokens contain identity and session information, not an authoritative permanent copy of every permission.
- Authorization checks consult current state or a tightly controlled, versioned cache.
- Revocation events invalidate relevant cached permissions.
- Sensitive operations can require a fresh authorization check.
This is especially important for administrative accounts.

6. Scope architecture — where exactly can a responsibility operate?
A role without a scope is dangerous.

Consider someone who is a HOD.

Do they have authority over:
- The entire institution?
- One school?
- One department?
- Several departments?
- A department at one campus?
- All historical records?
- Current records only?
The role name alone cannot answer these questions.
6.1 Scope types
We should initially support:
Scope	Example
Tenant	Platform-level tenant administration
Institution	Institution-wide administration
Institution group	Authorized group-level operations
Campus	One physical/operational campus
School	One academic school
Department	One department
Organizational unit	Placement or examination office
Cohort	Batch 2027
Program	B.Tech CSE
Section	CSE-A
Resource	One specific placement drive
Self	The user's own records
Assigned resources	Records explicitly allocated to the user

Some of these scopes originate in CAMP-01 or CAMP-03, while others are defined by business domains.

Authorization should resolve references through their owning domains.
6.2 Scope inheritance
This is a major decision.

If someone is assigned the role HOD for School A, should they automatically access every department in that school?

Sometimes yes. Sometimes no.

I recommend that scope inheritance must be explicit in the role or permission definition.

For example:
Role	Scope	Inheritance
Principal	Institution	Approved institution-wide permissions
HOD	Department	Department only
Dean	School	Approved child academic units
TPO	Institution	Institution-wide placement operations
Placement Coordinator	Department	Assigned department only
Faculty	Assigned resources	Explicit courses and classes
Student	Self	Own records

A role's administrative position must not automatically imply permission inheritance.
6.3 Prevent upward permission inheritance
An important safeguard:

A person assigned permissions over CSE must not automatically gain permission over its parent School of Engineering.

Likewise, an HOD should not automatically inherit authority over the principal's office or other departments.

We should support only approved inheritance directions, typically from a higher scope to its explicitly permitted descendants.

Upward inheritance should be disabled by default.
6.4 Matrix organizations
Our scope system must handle structures where people have responsibilities across several domains.

Example:

A central TPO may manage placement operations for all campuses but have no access to examination marks.

A campus TPO may manage placement drives for their campus.

A department coordinator might handle registration and student communication only for their department.

These can be represented by separate assignments:
Role: Central TPO
Scope: Institution
Permissions:
    placement.drive.manage
    placement.company.manage
    placement.report.institution.read

Role: Placement Coordinator
Scope: CSE Department
Permissions:
    placement.student.registration.read
    placement.communication.manage
    placement.department.report.read


The second person does not receive the central TPO's institution-wide permissions.

7. Permission catalogue — define atomic capabilities
This is where we stop relying on vague role labels.

Instead of asking whether someone is an administrator, the system asks whether they can perform a specific operation on a specific resource.

I suggest:

The permission catalogue should be owned and versioned centrally.

Institution administrators can select approved capabilities when defining custom roles, but cannot invent arbitrary permission semantics.
7.2 Separate operation types
A major mistake would be treating READ and WRITE as sufficient.

We need distinct actions such as:
Action	Meaning
Read	View resource
Create	Create a resource
Update	Modify permitted fields
Delete	Remove where allowed
Publish	Make content available
Approve	Approve an operation
Reject	Reject a request
Assign	Assign a responsibility
Revoke	Remove authority
Export	Extract data
Verify	Confirm authenticity
Delegate	Transfer defined authority

Permissions should be as granular as operational risk requires, but not so granular that maintaining them becomes impossible.
7.3 Field-level authorization
Suppose a faculty member can read a student profile.

That does not mean they should automatically be able to see:
- Private contact information.
- Disciplinary records.
- Financial details.
- Identity verification documents.
- Confidential counselling information.
- Restricted recruiter feedback.
We should support property-level visibility policies in addition to resource-level permission checks.

Some records should be in separate domains or restricted resources altogether.

For example, a student profile's basic educational information might be available to an authorized placement coordinator, while confidential grievance information remains restricted to the appropriate student-affairs personnel.

A permission to view an object is not automatically a permission to read every field within it.

8. Authorization engine — the combination that makes everything work
I recommend a hybrid authorization system.

Pure RBAC becomes restrictive when departments, cohorts, committees and temporary assignments interact.

Pure ABAC can become difficult to administer because every decision becomes a collection of attributes and complex policies.

Relationship-based access control is useful for relationships such as:
- A faculty member mentors this student.
- An interviewer is assigned to this interview.
- A coordinator owns this drive.
- An advisor is responsible for this cohort.
We should combine the models.
8.1 The authorization model
Hybrid authorization architecture
RBAC

Roles and their assigned permissions


ABAC

Identity, resource and contextual attributes


ReBAC

Relationships between people and resources




Policy Decision Point

Combines relevant policies and context


Decision
ALLOW

DENY

REVIEW




The critical difference is that roles provide baseline capabilities, but actual authorization also depends on scope, relationships and context.
8.2 Use the standard authorization components
I recommend structuring the engine around these components:
Component	Responsibility
PEP — Policy Enforcement Point	Intercepts protected operations
PDP — Policy Decision Point	Makes authorization decision
PAP — Policy Administration Point	Manages policies and permission definitions
PIP — Policy Information Point	Supplies identity, membership, resource and relationship attributes

This creates clean boundaries.

For example, a placement API is a PEP. It sends an authorization request to the PDP. The PDP gathers current role assignments and relevant contextual information from the PIP. The PDP returns the decision.
8.3 Authorization request format
Conceptually:
{
  "subject": {
    "account_id": "ACC001",
    "person_id": "PER001",
    "tenant_id": "TEN001",
    "institution_id": "INST001",
    "membership_id": "MEM001"
  },
  "action": "placement.application.view",
  "resource": {
    "type": "application",
    "id": "APP001",
    "institution_id": "INST001",
    "department_id": "DEPT001"
  },
  "context": {
    "session_assurance": "MFA",
    "request_channel": "WEB",
    "request_time": "2026-11-04T10:00:00Z"
  }
}


The exact fields should be derived from authenticated server context and trusted resource metadata.

A frontend must never be trusted to tell the server:
{
  "role": "TPO",
  "department_id": "CSE"
}


Those claims must be resolved from authoritative server-side records.
8.4 Authorization evaluation algorithm
My recommended conceptual sequence:
1. Authenticate the principal
        |
2. Resolve active account and session
        |
3. Establish trusted tenant context
        |
4. Resolve institutional membership
        |
5. Validate membership status
        |
6. Resolve active role assignments
        |
7. Resolve applicable permission definitions
        |
8. Resolve role scope and inheritance
        |
9. Evaluate resource relationships
        |
10. Evaluate contextual and attribute policies
        |
11. Apply explicit deny and security constraints
        |
12. Return decision and reason codes
        |
13. Record decision where required


The order is intentional.

For instance, even if a role assignment exists, a suspended membership should normally prevent its use.

The overall policy should follow:
- Explicit deny overrides ordinary allow.
- No matching allow means deny.
- Invalid or unavailable authorization context means deny for protected actions.
- A review-required state must never be silently treated as allow.
- Break-glass access must follow its separate policy.
8.5 A critical distinction: Authorization policy versus business policy
Remember CAMP-01's rule engine?

We should not merge it with the authorization engine.
Authorization policy	Business policy
Can this person publish a drive?	Can this student register for a drive?
Can this HOD view departmental results?	Does this student satisfy the CGPA cutoff?
Can a coordinator approve an exception?	Which exceptions are permitted?
Can an administrator assign an HOD role?	What makes a student eligible for graduation?

Both can evaluate structured policies, but they have different owners, security implications, audit requirements and lifecycles.

This separation is non-negotiable.


9. Delegation architecture — temporary authority without permanent role changes
This is an essential requirement for actual institutional operations.

Consider these situations:
- A TPO is on leave and assigns responsibilities to another coordinator.
- A HOD temporarily delegates approval authority.
- A principal authorizes a deputy to approve certain institutional operations.
- A placement coordinator transfers responsibility for a particular recruitment drive.
- A faculty member appoints another faculty member as a temporary evaluator.
We should not create permanent role assignments just to handle temporary substitutions.

We need a dedicated delegation system.

9.2 Delegation constraints
A delegation must:
1. Never exceed the original authority.
2. Never exceed the original scope.
3. Never last beyond the original assignment's validity.
4. Support explicit permission exclusions.
5. Be auditable.
6. Be immediately revocable.
7. Prevent unauthorized further delegation.
8. Respect separation-of-duties rules.
Example:

A TPO has permission to publish placement drives across the institution.

They delegate permission to manage registration and scheduling for one drive to a coordinator.

The coordinator should not receive the TPO's other institutional permissions.
9.3 Sub-delegation
My recommendation is to disable sub-delegation by default.

If allowed for a particular responsibility, it must be explicitly configured and bounded by the original delegation.
9.4 Delegation is not impersonation
The delegate acts under their own account.

The audit trail should show:
- Original authority holder.
- Delegate.
- Delegated operation.
- Delegation reference.
- Resource and scope.
- Time of execution.
We must never obscure who actually performed an operation.

10. Separation of duties and approval governance
A powerful authorization system must prevent conflicts of interest, not just grant capabilities.

Consider:
- A person creating and approving their own institutional admin assignment.
- A result-entry operator approving their own published results.
- A person submitting and approving their own financial transaction.
- A recruiter evaluating a close relative.
- A platform support employee changing their own privileges.
We need separation-of-duties controls.
10.1 Four types of separation
Control	Meaning
Static separation	Certain roles cannot be held simultaneously
Dynamic separation	Certain responsibilities cannot be exercised in the same transaction
Approval separation	Requester and approver must be different where configured
Contextual separation	Access is blocked because of a specific conflict or relationship

Example:

A role combination might be allowed, but the person cannot use both roles to initiate and approve the same transaction.

This is often more practical than banning the role combination altogether.
10.2 Approval policy
For sensitive operations, define separate stages:
Request
   |
Validation
   |
Approval assignment
   |
Independent approval
   |
Activation
   |
Audit and notification


The approval workflow should be configurable, but important operations should have platform-enforced safeguards.

For example, creating an institutional administrator can require:
- Verified person identity.
- Existing authorized requester.
- Independent approval.
- MFA.
- Explicit assignment scope.
- Audit event.
10.3 Conflict-of-interest declarations
For certain workflows, the system can incorporate declared conflicts of interest.

For example, an examiner should not evaluate a candidate when an applicable conflict has been declared.

The domain workflow should supply the conflict information, while CAMP-02 enforces the required access restriction.

We should not use the authorization engine as the only conflict-management system.

11. Privileged access architecture
Not all users should be treated equally.

A student logging in to view their application is very different from an institution administrator modifying a role or a platform operator accessing customer data.

We need separate protection for privileged accounts and operations.
11.1 Privilege tiers
Tier	Example	Controls
P0	Regular student	Standard authentication
P1	Faculty and coordinators	MFA based on risk and operation
P2	Department/institution administrators	Strong MFA and sensitive-operation verification
P3	Principal, registrar, institution super-admin	Phishing-resistant MFA preferred, privileged audit
P4	Platform operations and security admins	Dedicated privileged accounts, just-in-time access, monitored actions

These are security tiers, not academic ranks.

The role and the privilege tier should be separate properties.
11.2 Just-in-time privileged access
Rather than giving platform operators permanent access to every institutional workspace:
Support employee requests access
        |
Purpose and case reference
        |
Approval
        |
Temporary privilege activation
        |
Restricted access window
        |
Activity monitoring and audit
        |
Automatic expiration


This makes customer support and operational administration safer.
11.3 Break-glass access
We also need an emergency mechanism.

Suppose an institution administrator's account becomes inaccessible during a critical operation.

A properly controlled emergency access process should allow designated trusted individuals to restore essential access.

But:
- It must have a documented emergency reason.
- It must have limited permissions.
- It must trigger immediate alerts.
- It must expire automatically.
- All actions must be independently reviewed.
- It must not become an ordinary administrative shortcut.
Break-glass access should never mean an unlogged global superuser account.

12. Session and authentication architecture
We have discussed authorization, but authentication also needs a proper design.

The system should support institutional SSO, local authentication and federated identities.
12.1 Authentication architecture
Identity authentication pipeline
Platform login

Password, passkey, MFA


Institution SSO

OIDC, SAML or approved provider




Identity provider validation

Issuer, subject, signature, audience, expiry and authentication assurance


Account resolution

Find or securely link the internal account


Session creation

Account, tenant context, authentication strength and session risk


Authorization context established


Authentication and authorization should remain different services.

A successful login proves control of an authentication identity. It does not prove that the person is authorized to access a particular institution.
12.2 Multiple authentication methods
We should support:
- Email/password where enabled.
- Passkeys.
- TOTP-based MFA.
- Hardware security keys.
- Institution-controlled SSO.
- Recovery codes.
- Authorized recovery procedures.
SMS OTP can be supported as a recovery or lower-assurance method where appropriate, but should not be our only strong authentication mechanism for administrators.

We should never require every student to own an institutional email address to have a platform identity.
12.3 Session management
Session should record:
Field	Description
session_id	Session identifier
account_id	Authenticated account
tenant_context	Current tenant context
identity_provider	Authentication source
auth_time	Authentication timestamp
auth_assurance	Authentication strength
created_at	Session creation
last_activity_at	Last activity
expires_at	Expiration
revoked_at	Revocation timestamp

A user can have several active sessions.

A student might use a laptop and phone simultaneously.

But switching institutions should require establishing a valid institutional context, not trusting a client-supplied institution ID.
12.4 Session and role changes
Suppose a faculty member's HOD assignment is revoked.

The platform should:
1. Commit the revocation.
2. Publish an authorization invalidation event.
3. Invalidate relevant access caches.
4. Make subsequent protected operations fail.
5. Revoke affected sessions where risk warrants it.
We should not necessarily terminate every session merely because one role changed; that depends on the active session context and security level.

High-risk account changes, such as account disablement or compromised authentication, should trigger wider session revocation.

13. Temporary actors and special identities
An institution is not only composed of students and employees.

There are visitors, external examiners, auditors, guest faculty, parents, recruiters and service integrations.

The system must accommodate them without turning them into regular faculty or students.
13.1 External membership
Example:

An external examiner needs access to three specific examination records for two weeks.

We create:
- An account.
- A person identity if the actor is human.
- A temporary external membership.
- A narrowly scoped role assignment.
- A time-limited access grant.
- A resource-specific authorization rule.
When the assignment expires, access automatically stops.
13.2 Parent and guardian identities
This is a special case worth treating carefully.

A parent or guardian should not automatically see a student's complete profile merely because they have a relationship with the student.

We need:
- Verified guardian relationship.
- Student age and applicable legal context.
- Institutional policy.
- Specific data-sharing permissions.
- Consent or other appropriate legal basis.
- Revocation and expiry.
- Audited access.
The student lifecycle and privacy domains will own the underlying relationship and consent facts. CAMP-02 will enforce the applicable access constraints.
13.3 Service identities
We need separate non-human principals for integrations.

Examples:
- Student ERP sync.
- Examination result import.
- Attendance connector.
- Institutional SSO integration.
- Bulk document import.
A service identity should have:
- One tenant scope.
- Explicit API permissions.
- Limited resource access.
- Credential rotation.
- Request quotas.
- Expiry or revocation.
- Audit history.
Service accounts must never inherit human administrative roles.

14.4 Database schema example
Let's implement a small part of this properly.

Institutional membership
CREATE TABLE access.institutional_membership (
    tenant_id UUID NOT NULL,
    institution_id UUID NOT NULL,
    id UUID NOT NULL,

    account_id UUID NOT NULL,
    person_id UUID NOT NULL,

    membership_type VARCHAR(40) NOT NULL,
    status VARCHAR(30) NOT NULL DEFAULT 'INVITED',

    valid_from TIMESTAMPTZ,
    valid_to TIMESTAMPTZ,

    source_domain VARCHAR(50),
    source_record_id UUID,

    created_at TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL DEFAULT NOW(),

    PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, institution_id, id),

    FOREIGN KEY (tenant_id, institution_id)
        REFERENCES institution.institution(
            tenant_id, id
        ),

    FOREIGN KEY (account_id)
        REFERENCES access.account(id),

    FOREIGN KEY (person_id)
        REFERENCES identity.person(id),

    CHECK (
        valid_to IS NULL OR
        valid_from IS NULL OR
        valid_to > valid_from
    )
);


The application should additionally verify that the account and person are correctly linked and that the account is permitted to join the tenant. We should not rely on separate independent foreign keys for that relationship.

Role assignment
CREATE TABLE authorization.role_assignment (
    tenant_id UUID NOT NULL,
    institution_id UUID NOT NULL,
    id UUID NOT NULL,

    membership_id UUID NOT NULL,
    role_version_id UUID NOT NULL,

    scope_type VARCHAR(50) NOT NULL,
    scope_id UUID NOT NULL,

    status VARCHAR(30) NOT NULL DEFAULT 'PENDING',

    valid_from TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL,
    valid_to TIMESTAMPTZ,

    assigned_by_account_id UUID NOT NULL,
    approved_by_account_id UUID,

    reason TEXT,

    version BIGINT NOT NULL DEFAULT 1,

    created_at TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL DEFAULT NOW(),

    PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, institution_id, id),

    FOREIGN KEY (
        tenant_id, institution_id, membership_id
    )
    REFERENCES access.institutional_membership(
        tenant_id, institution_id, id
    ),

    CHECK (
        valid_to IS NULL OR
        valid_to > valid_from
    )
);


This is a conceptual starting DDL, not yet production-ready.

In production, scope_type + scope_id should be resolved through validated typed scope references. We should not accept arbitrary identifiers simply because the UUID is syntactically valid.

Similarly, role versions, permission references and actor references require tenant-aware foreign keys or properly defined cross-schema ownership constraints.
14.5 Role scope implementation
I would introduce a ScopeRegistry interface rather than creating foreign keys from every authorization table to every possible scope table.

It can resolve approved types such as:
TENANT
INSTITUTION
CAMPUS
INSTITUTION_GROUP
ORGANIZATIONAL_UNIT
ACADEMIC_UNIT
PROGRAM
COHORT
RESOURCE
SELF


The owning domain validates the resource's existence and tenant/institution association.

This avoids creating circular database dependencies between CAMP-01, CAMP-02, CAMP-03 and later placement or examination domains.

However, a generic UUID field is not enough for integrity. Each scope type needs its own resolver, validation rules and ownership contract.

15. Identity onboarding and provisioning pipelines
Now we must define how people actually enter the system.

There will be multiple sources:
- Institutional administrator invitations.
- Bulk student imports.
- ERP synchronization.
- Faculty and staff imports.
- Self-registration.
- Alumni activation.
- External examiner invitations.
- Parent or guardian invitations.
- SSO-based account provisioning.
All of these should feed a common identity provisioning pipeline.
15.1 Account and membership creation pipeline
Identity provisioning pipeline
01. Identity source

Manual invitation, ERP, SSO, CSV or registration request


02. Identity resolution

Find existing account/person, check trusted identity evidence and possible duplicates


03. Account validation

Validate authentication identity, provider and account status


04. Membership creation

Create institution-scoped membership and bind the source affiliation record


05. Role provisioning

Assign only approved baseline roles, scoped responsibilities and permissions


06. Activation and notification

Activate membership when prerequisites are met and deliver secure account access instructions


07. Domain events

AccountProvisioned · MembershipActivated · RoleAssigned


15.2 Bulk student provisioning
Suppose an institution uploads 8,000 student records.

We should not simply create 8,000 independent accounts and send 8,000 emails without understanding identity duplication.

Instead:
1. Receive the student import batch.
2. Validate tenant and institution ownership.
3. Match existing person records using approved identifiers.
4. Identify possible duplicate identities.
5. Create or link accounts.
6. Create institutional memberships.
7. Receive or reference authoritative student enrollment records.
8. Assign the correct baseline student role.
9. Activate access according to institutional policy.
10. Send invitations or enable institutional SSO.
Possible cases:
Case	Expected result
New student, no account	Create person, account and membership
Existing platform account	Reuse account after verified linking
Existing person with different institution	Add separate membership
Same institutional registration number	Prevent duplicate enrollment-based membership
Possible duplicate identity	Hold for identity resolution
Missing authentication contact	Allow controlled institution-managed activation
Student already graduated	Use appropriate alumni transition
Disabled account	Do not reactivate without authorized review

An important distinction: identity matching and student record matching are different operations.

Two students can have similar names and matching initials without being the same person.

We should prefer institution-issued unique identifiers combined with trusted evidence. Ambiguous matches must go into review.

16. Authorization execution pipeline
Let's make the runtime process concrete.

Suppose a CSE placement coordinator tries to view a student from the ECE department.

What should happen?

We need a controlled decision.

16.2 Decision output
Our authorization service should return a structured result.
{
  "decision_id": "DEC001",
  "decision": "DENY",
  "reason_code": "SCOPE_MISMATCH",
  "subject_id": "ACC001",
  "action": "student.profile.read",
  "resource_id": "STU001",
  "policy_version": "AUTHZ_V4",
  "evaluated_at": "2026-11-04T10:00:00Z"
}


For a denial, we should reveal enough information to explain the error without disclosing confidential resource details.

Internally, the audit record can contain richer diagnostics.
16.3 Authorization cache
We need high performance, so checking every role assignment with multiple database joins on every operation may eventually become expensive.

But caching authorization blindly is dangerous.

I recommend:
- Cache role and permission resolution using tenant, membership and scope.
- Include role-assignment versions.
- Include policy versions.
- Use short, controlled cache validity.
- Invalidate on role changes and revocation.
- Apply stricter cache policies to high-risk permissions.
- Re-check sensitive actions against current authoritative state.
Never cache an ALLOW decision indefinitely.

Also, authorization caches must not be shared across tenants without tenant-aware namespacing.
16.4 Authorization checkpoints
Not every request needs the same level of reevaluation.
Operation	Required check
View general institutional directory	Standard
View own student profile	Standard
View another student's personal data	Membership + resource scope
Export 5,000 student records	Fresh permission + export policy
Publish examination results	Fresh authorization + approval state
Assign institution administrator	Privileged verification + independent approval
Modify institutional structure	Current role + concurrency/approval validation
Access confidential records	Fresh authorization and enhanced audit

The security model should scale its checks with the sensitivity and impact of the operation.

18. Real institutional scenarios — test the model before implementation
Let's test our design against complicated combinations.

These are not hypothetical edge cases we should postpone. They are the kinds of combinations we should design the system to handle.
Scenario A — Central TPO and departmental coordinators
An institution has four campuses and twelve departments.

The responsibilities are:
Person	Role	Scope
Person A	Central TPO	Institution
Person B	Campus TPO	Campus North
Person C	Placement Coordinator	CSE, Campus North
Person D	Placement Coordinator	ECE, Campus North
Person E	Placement Coordinator	CSE, Campus South

The system should allow:
- Person A to oversee institution-wide placement operations.
- Person B to manage campus-level placement operations.
- Person C to manage CSE placement activities in Campus North.
- Person D to manage ECE placement activities in Campus North.
- Person E to manage CSE placement activities in Campus South.
But the scope hierarchy alone must not decide everything.

For example, Person B might view placement reports for all departments in Campus North but not modify their eligibility policies.

A person's role must resolve to specific permissions.
Scenario B — Faculty member who is also HOD and examiner
A faculty member receives three assignments.
Assignment	Scope	Permission examples
Faculty	Assigned courses	Attendance and course operations
HOD	CSE department	Departmental administration
Examiner	Specific exam	Enter and evaluate marks

When the examination assignment expires, the other two assignments remain active.

This is why roles must be independently assigned and revoked.
Scenario C — Student representative
A student has:
- Student membership.
- Student role.
- Student representative assignment.
The representative can:
- Submit departmental requests.
- View aggregated student feedback.
- Coordinate authorized events.
- Submit approved announcements for review.
The representative cannot automatically:
- View other students' complete records.
- Edit attendance.
- Change examination results.
- Approve placement eligibility.
- Access confidential grievances.
A custom student role should not imply access to administrative records merely because the student represents a cohort.
Scenario D — Faculty transfers between departments
Suppose a faculty member transfers from CSE to ECE.

The correct process is:
Old academic/staff assignment closed
        |
Old department-scoped role assignments reviewed
        |
Required role assignments revoked or transitioned
        |
New department assignment recorded
        |
New scoped responsibilities granted
        |
Authorization caches invalidated
        |
Historical access preserved


We should not automatically transfer every old permission to the new department.
Scenario E — One person across two institutions
Person A is an alumnus of Institution X and a faculty member at Institution Y.

They should have:
- One linked platform identity where appropriately verified.
- Separate institutional memberships.
- Separate role assignments.
- Separate access contexts.
- No automatic visibility between the two institutions.
They should not have to log out of one institution to use the other, but their active authorization context must be explicit.
Scenario F — Temporary principal delegation
The principal is away for a week.

A deputy receives temporary authority to approve institutional announcements and selected operational matters.

The deputy does not become principal, and their account must continue to identify them as the actual actor.

The authority is created as a delegation, has a clear expiry and can be revoked immediately.
Scenario G — Institution administrator leaves
An administrator's employment ends.

The institution terminates their employment relationship and initiates the corresponding membership and role-revocation process.

The account may remain active for other valid institutional or personal platform memberships.

However, the former administrator must lose the institution's administrative access immediately.

This is a critical example of why Account, Person, Membership and Role Assignment cannot be the same record.

19. Security, compliance and privacy architecture
Identity and access management must be designed with security as a foundational requirement.

We should include the following controls.
Area	Controls
Account security	Credential protection, secure recovery, account lockout
Authentication	MFA, SSO, passkeys
Authorization	Least privilege, scoped permissions, deny by default
Privileged access	Just-in-time access, approvals, strong authentication
Sessions	Revocation, expiry and session management
Identity linking	Verified association and duplicate-resolution controls
Audit	Immutable or tamper-evident security history
Data privacy	Purpose-based visibility, data minimisation
Integration security	Scoped service identities and credential rotation
Abuse detection	Suspicious access and abnormal activity detection

19.1 Protection against privilege escalation
We should explicitly protect against:
- Assigning oneself administrative permissions.
- Creating a custom role with unrestricted permissions.
- Delegating more authority than one possesses.
- Accessing a different institution by changing a request parameter.
- Reusing revoked tokens.
- Using a service account as a human administrator.
- Manipulating scope IDs.
- Exploiting cached authorization decisions after revocation.
- Creating an administrator through bulk import without authorized approval.
19.2 Privacy-aware access
We should make access decisions based on both authorization and data sensitivity.

For example:

An HOD may have access to departmental academic progress but not automatically to private counselling records.

An interviewer may view the candidate information required for an interview but should not have general access to the institution's student database.

A parent may have a verified relationship with a student, but still only see the data covered by valid access permissions.
19.3 Indian compliance design
We should design identity and access processing to support applicable Indian privacy requirements, including the Digital Personal Data Protection framework.

In particular, account creation, identity verification, communication and information sharing should not be treated as blanket consent for every future institutional use.

The platform needs purpose-specific processing records, transparent privacy controls, appropriate retention, security safeguards and workflows for applicable individual rights.

The precise legal basis and obligations should be reviewed for each processing context rather than assuming every operation requires the same consent mechanism.

Revoked TPO uses old session for sensitive operation	Deny
Administrator attempts self-approval of privileged assignment	Reject
Guest membership expires	Deny subsequent protected operations
Two administrators simultaneously modify the same assignment	Prevent lost update
Same provisioning command is retried	No duplicate membership
Institutional SSO subject changes unexpectedly	Require controlled re-linking
Directory lookup reveals another tenant's membership	Deny

20.3 Authorization performance requirements
We should make performance measurable rather than saying the service must be fast.

Initial engineering targets for load testing could be:
Operation	Initial target
Cached standard authorization decision	p95 under 50 ms
Database-backed authorization decision	p95 under 150 ms
Bulk role provisioning	Asynchronous
Revocation propagation	Under 5 seconds
Privileged access validation	Fresh authoritative check

These are provisional targets, not measured guarantees. We should validate them against realistic workloads, infrastructure and security requirements.

Most importantly, revocation correctness takes priority over cache performance.

Some details need a larger platform-wide design.
Decision	Reason

I would not rush to introduce a dedicated authorization graph database or a separate authorization microservice.

We can implement the logical authorization boundary within our modular backend and extract it when operational scale, independent deployment or security isolation justifies the move.

My recommendation for the next stage
We should design CAMP-03 with the same depth, particularly because it will become the authoritative foundation for student records and placement eligibility.

I want to make sure it supports different Indian academic systems without assuming every university follows the same semester structure, grading conventions or progression rules.

One additional principle: CAMP-01 and CAMP-02 are currently architecture specifications, not deployed software. Once we finish the domain design, we'll consolidate these specifications into an implementation-ready technical blueprint, including migrations, API contracts, event schemas, test cases and architecture decision records.

We are building an institutional platform whose foundations should survive years of new modules, thousands of institutional configurations and millions of users—not merely a placement application that happens to work today.
